In Defense
of the Open
Loop

Or: some unasked-for thoughts on why the itch is the whole goddamn point

Filed under: things your productivity coach got catastrophically wrong

They want you finished.

Every last one of them — the productivity gurus with their Notion templates and their morning routines and their fourteen-step systems for achieving a state of mind so resolved, so efficiently emptied, that it barely qualifies as consciousness. Close your loops, they say. Inbox zero. Get to done. As if the whole gorgeous, overcrowded, occasionally deranged machine of human thought exists so you can feel briefly smug at the end of a Tuesday and go to bed at a reasonable hour with a clear conscience and absolutely nothing to show for it.

What a colossal fucking waste.

Here is what nobody will tell you about an open loop: it has heat. It follows you. It shows up in the shower and sits down next to you at dinner uninvited and presses its knee against yours under the table and does not look away. That is not a problem to be eliminated with a system. That is your mind telling you it's still interested — that something here isn't done, that the thing has more to give, that you would be a goddamn fool to close the tab.

The brain grips an open
problem the same way
it grips desire.
On purpose.

The Zeigarnik crowd frames this as pathology. Cognitive drag. A tax on your attention, a mess to clean up, a symptom of insufficient systematizing. They love their little waiter story — look, he remembered the orders until he didn't need to anymore, isn't that instructive. But read what the story is actually saying: the brain will not release something it finds important. It locks on. It keeps it warm and tense and close to the surface. It wakes up at 3am still turning the thing over, still looking for the angle, still faintly aroused by the possibility that there is something there, some combination, some crack in the door, that nobody has found yet.

You want to close that? File it, rinse your hands, and move on to the next deliverable? That's not discipline. That's pulling out early and calling it strategy. The problem was getting somewhere. You cut it off. The subconscious, which unlike you is neither impatient nor easily distracted, had been building toward something in the dark — and you walked in, declared victory, and filed the whole unfinished business under RESOLVED.

You resolved nothing. You just stopped paying attention.

*   *   *

Graham Wallas figured this out in 1926 and called it incubation — the second stage of creative thought, the fallow period after you stop forcing it, when the problem goes underground and starts doing its real work without your help or your interference. The research on this is solid and largely ignored by people selling productivity frameworks, which should tell you something about the productivity framework industry.

The subconscious is a relentless little bastard. It doesn't clock out. It doesn't need your permission or your encouragement or your carefully maintained second brain. It will keep grinding, in the dark, on whatever you've left open — making connections that your conscious mind is far too proud and too rushed and too in love with its own cleverness to make on its own.

But here is the catch, and it is a fatal one for the loop-closers: you cannot incubate a closed loop. The moment you file it, resolve it, relay it to the chef — the subconscious drops it. That is exactly what the mechanism predicts. Completion triggers release. So the guru selling you the system of perfect closure is, with great efficiency and zero self-awareness, firing your best employee. The one who works nights for free. The one who was six inches from something. Gone, because you needed the dopamine hit of a checked box before lunch.

Wallas, 1926. Four stages: preparation, incubation, illumination, verification. The productivity gospel skips incubation entirely and wonders why its insights are thin. You cannot harvest what you will not let grow.
*   *   *

I have known men with clean desks.

A specific kind of clean — the aggressive kind, the kind that requires maintenance. Inbox at zero by end of day. Every loop closed. Every question answered, deferred, or killed. They smell like corporate soap and low-grade disappointment. They use words like "bandwidth" and "action items" without a trace of irony. They are polished in a way that suggests nothing genuinely strange or difficult has happened to them in years.

They have never let an idea run long enough to get weird. Never held a question open through three conversations, two walks, one bad night, until it cracked open at a moment they didn't plan and spilled something unexpected all over the floor. They have not suffered in the directions that produce interesting thinking. Their loops are closed, their nights are calm, their desks are immaculate.

They bore me to death. Every single one of them.
*   *   *

Here is the distinction that never gets made, and it's the only one that matters: there is the open loop that drains you and the open loop that animates you, and these are not the same animal.

The draining loop is an obligation you're avoiding — an email you owe someone, a call you keep rescheduling, a decision you're too gutless to make. Yes, close those. They deserve nothing better. Close them and feel nothing.

But the animating loop — the one with heat, the one pressing its knee against yours — is not an obligation. It is a desire. It is the specific, exquisite discomfort of something not yet resolved, not yet spent, still live and hungry and looking for its ending. Foreplay has a terrible reputation among people who want to get things done. But foreplay is where the intelligence lives. It's where the mind tests angles, follows threads, learns what the problem actually wants from you, discovers where it's soft. The sprint to the finish is just mechanics. Anyone can sprint.

Don't scratch the itch.
Let it burn.
That's how you know
it means something.

Raise a child. Love someone for twenty years. Write a book that doesn't embarrass you. Try to say one true and original thing about the world before you die. Not one of these will ever be finished. Not one of them should be. The open loop isn't a failure of your system. It's proof you've attached yourself to something larger and more stubborn than your to-do list — something that will not be filed, that will not stay resolved, that keeps coming back because it isn't done with you yet.

So let them pile up. Let them run loose at two in the morning, throwing heat, making noise, crashing into each other. Sit with the want. Don't rush the ending. The discomfort of the unfinished is not a symptom to be treated.

It's the proof of concept. It's the whole fucking thing.
